## Research context

Global normative guidance, intended for national adaptation, on how to design, govern, communicate and evaluate a heat–health warning system (HHWS). It is the primary joint reference from the two agencies that hold the formal mandates over the weather component and the health component respectively. It prescribes practice rather than measuring it — there are no effect sizes in this document.

## Stakeholders

National Meteorological and Hydrological Services (NMHSs); health and social service sectors; emergency response agencies; media; community organizations; employers; at-risk subgroups including outdoor workers.

## Contact points

- Met service → health authority (forecast and threshold-crossing notification)
- Alert issuer → emergency-response agencies (operational message)
- Alert issuer → media (public-facing message, different format)
- Health/social services → community organizations → at-risk individuals
- Employers → outdoor workers (worker monitoring, hydration, schedule modification)
- A standing cross-disciplinary "Community of Practice" (§5.4) as the persistent inter-agency contact point outside events

## Information transferred

Forecast and observed heat conditions; the threshold crossing itself; the graded alert level; bespoke action content differentiated by recipient type; timing ("the when, what, how and to whom" of heat-related messaging, Executive Summary p. xii).

## Channels

The guidance treats channel choice as a design decision rather than prescribing a channel list, and separates coordination with emergency-response agencies from coordination with media as **separate communication tasks requiring different message formats** (§5.3.1–5.3.2).

## Handoffs and ownership

The foundational ownership split for the entire project: **the climate component is owned by National Meteorological and Hydrological Services and the societal-response component is owned by the health and social service sectors** (p. vii). Threshold-setting is explicitly a *joint* met–health decision because "HHWS thresholds are response-specific… set at a level associated with a negative human response" (p. xii). Alert *issuance* (Ch. 4) and alert *dissemination* (Ch. 5) are formally separate stages with different owners.

## Acknowledgement or escalation

Escalation is handled through "a system of graded alerts/actions for communication to the general population" (p. xi) — the graded alert level is the escalation device. This document does **not** specify an acknowledgement mechanism; for that requirement see CS-14.

## Failure points

The guidance states plainly that "without an effective communication and dissemination strategy, the benefits arising from an HHWS…may be minimal." It requires "clear, unambiguous language" and "bespoke messages, which may be action-threshold specific (health authority, emergency service, media, community action group)" — implying that a single undifferentiated message to all recipients is a recognised failure mode.

## Adoption or satisfaction findings

None empirically. The document does, however, establish the evaluation frame this project should adopt: "Evaluations fall into two broad types, namely process- and outcome-based", and evaluation design "should be an integral part of the early development phase of an HHWS."

## Relevance to this project

This is the source that legitimises the HeatShield boundary. It confirms that a heat-risk service is *not* one organisation's system but a handoff between a meteorological owner and a health/social-response owner, and that the dashboard sits at the seam. It also pre-authorises the project's evaluation approach — a process-based evaluation designed in from the start is exactly what WHO/WMO expect at this stage, not an outcome claim.

## Limitations

Guidance, not evaluation: it describes intended rather than observed arrangements. No formal memorandum-of-understanding or legal instruments are elaborated in the retrieved text. The 2015 vintage predates widespread smartphone and cell-broadcast alerting, so its channel discussion is dated.

## Supported claims

1. A heat–health warning system splits into a meteorological component owned by the met service and a societal-response component owned by health and social services (p. vii).
2. Heat-risk detection is owned by the meteorological service, while threshold-setting is a joint met–health decision because thresholds are response-specific (p. xii).
3. Alert issuance and alert dissemination are formally separate stages with different owners (Ch. 4 vs Ch. 5).
4. Graded alert levels are the standard escalation device for public communication (p. xi).
5. Messages should be bespoke to recipient type, not identical across all recipients.
6. Formal evaluation of warning-system performance is an expected, designed-in stage, and falls into process-based and outcome-based types (Ch. 7).
