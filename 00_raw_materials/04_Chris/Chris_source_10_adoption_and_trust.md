## Research context

Three studies that together quantify the gap between a warning being received and a person doing something about it. CS-13 is a 4-city telephone survey of 908 adults aged 65+ conducted within seven days of heat events (summers 2004–2005). CS-61 is 73 semi-structured interviews with people aged 72–94. CS-63 is a population-representative telephone survey of 801 NYC adults (September–October 2015).

## Stakeholders

Older adults; municipal health authorities; media as the dominant de facto channel.

## Contact points

Media-to-public is the dominant contact point in practice. In CS-13, television reached 64–92%, radio 10–40%, newspaper 9–38% and interpersonal contact only 2–17% — the official alert reached people mostly by being reported, not by being sent.

## Information transferred

The warning; and, much less successfully, the recommended action. CS-13 found message content collapsed in recall to three themes (avoid outdoors/sun, hydrate, be somewhere air-conditioned); **only 5% mentioned drinking more water unprompted versus 51% when directly asked.**

## Channels

See above. Advance notice was partial: a mean of **41% (29–50%)** had at least one day's advance knowledge.

## Handoffs and ownership

The referral handoff is where the chain visibly breaks. In CS-13 only **4–12%** cited cooling centres or hotlines, and **under 2% reported actually seeking a cooler location.**

## Acknowledgement or escalation

Not measured in any of the three.

## Failure points

1. **Self-exclusion from the risk category.** CS-61: **"Few respondents considered themselves either old or at risk from the effects of heat, even though many had some form of relevant chronic illness."** CS-13: more than half said heat was not, or only slightly, dangerous to them; only 15% felt it was "very dangerous" personally, despite all being 65+. CS-63: only **43% (95% CI 39–46)** believed indoor heat was likely to make them ill, against **68%** expressing general climate concern — abstract concern does not become personal risk.
2. **Cost of cooling.** CS-13: **"Over a third mentioned that economic factors of energy costs were considered in terms of how long or whether the air conditioner was turned on"** (35–47% by city). CS-63: 13% lacked functioning AC; 15% used it never or infrequently.
3. **Message confusion.** CS-13: in Toronto and Phoenix, heat precautions were conflated with air-pollution/ozone advice; 28% of fan users without AC kept windows closed.
4. **Distrust of the state as messenger.** CS-61: **"many thought state intervention was unnecessary, intrusive and unlikely to be effective. Respondents were more positive about the value of appropriately disseminated advice and solutions by communities themselves."**

## Adoption or satisfaction findings

**CS-13: "Knowledge of the heat warning was nearly universal (90%)"** (83–92% by city), but **only 46% (35–57%) changed their behaviour.**

**CS-63 gives the funnel with confidence intervals:** 66% (63–70) saw/heard/read warnings → 43% (39–46) believed indoor heat could make them ill → **12% (95% CI 8–16)** — see correction — reported going to a public air-conditioned place. Also 47% (41–53) stayed home despite the heat.

> **Verification correction (applied throughout the Chris deliverables):** the 12% figure is **not** measured cooling-centre use across the sample. The paper reports it as the proportion **of the subgroup who could not keep their home cool** who reported going to a public place with air conditioning. Do not paraphrase it as "12% of New Yorkers used cooling centres."

> **Note the contested denominator.** The widely repeated "~90% aware" figure comes from CS-13's 65+ sample. Erens et al. (2021, England, national — CS-28) found only **51.0% of all adults and 63.9% of the 75+ group** aware of hot-weather health publicity, and CS-63 found 66%. These measure different constructs in different populations. **Present the range 51–90% with the measurement differences stated; do not build the argument on 90% alone.**

## Relevance to this project

This is the empirical basis for the project's central claim that a dashboard which only improves *delivery* will not improve *outcomes*. It also identifies what HeatShield's evaluation must measure beyond reach: whether the user personalises the risk, whether they can recall the action unprompted, and whether the recommended action is affordable and available to them. The distrust finding (CS-61) argues for a community intermediary rather than a purely institutional voice.

## Limitations

CS-13: low response rate (**5.4% overall; ~2% in Toronto**), so self-selection risk is substantial; 2004–2005 data predates smartphone alerting, making the channel findings dated; self-reported behaviour; four cities; bespoke unvalidated survey items. CS-61: qualitative, n=73, two English locations, not statistically generalisable; 2009 data. CS-63: single city, self-report, 2015 fieldwork; the awareness question does not distinguish official alerts from media coverage; cross-sectional, so no causal inference. **CS-63 does not itself attribute low cooling-centre uptake to stigma — that link would be interpretation.**

## Supported claims

1. Knowledge of a heat warning was nearly universal (90%) among adults 65+ across four North American cities, but only 46% reported changing their behaviour (n=908, response rate 5.4%).
2. Few older respondents considered themselves old or at risk from heat, even when they had a relevant chronic illness (n=73, aged 72–94).
3. Many older respondents thought state intervention was unnecessary, intrusive and unlikely to be effective, and were more positive about advice disseminated by communities themselves.
4. In New York City, 66% reported seeing/hearing warnings, 43% believed indoor heat could make them ill, and 12% of those unable to keep their home cool reported going to a public air-conditioned place (n=801).
5. Over a third of respondents said energy cost affected whether or how long they ran air conditioning.
6. Recall of message content is poor without prompting: 5% mentioned drinking more water unprompted versus 51% when asked directly.
7. Only 4–12% recalled cooling centres or hotlines, and under 2% reported seeking a cooler location.
