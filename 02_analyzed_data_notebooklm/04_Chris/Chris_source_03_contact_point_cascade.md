# Chris Source 03 — A Named-Owner Alert Cascade in Practice (Ahmedabad)

**Focus applied to this summary:** stakeholders, communication logistics, contact points, channels, handoffs, ownership, acknowledgement, fallback mechanisms, adoption, satisfaction and service usefulness. Contact-point level only.

## Citation

Knowlton, K., Kulkarni, S.P., Azhar, G.S., Mavalankar, D., Jaiswal, A., Connolly, M., Nori-Sarma, A., Rajiva, A., Dutta, P., Deol, B., Sanchez, L., Khosla, R., Webster, P.J., Toma, V.E., Sheffield, P., Hess, J.J. & the Ahmedabad Heat and Climate Study Group (2014). Development and implementation of South Asia's first heat-health action plan in Ahmedabad (Gujarat, India). *International Journal of Environmental Research and Public Health*, 11(4), 3473–3492.
DOI: https://doi.org/10.3390/ijerph110403473
**Source ID:** CS-05 · **Source type:** Peer-reviewed article · **Full text:** YES (open access) · **Independently re-verified:** PASS.

## Research context

The first municipal heat–health action plan in South Asia, in a low-connectivity, high-heat, high-informality urban setting. It is the clearest published description anywhere in this review of a heat alert cascade with a **named individual owner** at the municipal hinge point — which is precisely the design question HeatShield faces.

## Stakeholders

Ahmedabad Municipal Corporation and its departments; urban health centres; hospitals; community link workers and ASHA workers; the India Meteorological Department; academic partners; an international NGO; media; schools; slum residents; outdoor workers.

## Contact points

1. Forecast provider → **AMC Nodal Officer** (a single named municipal individual), daily
2. Nodal Officer → Health Department
3. Health Department → local departments and community service providers
4. Community service providers → residents
5. Press office → media → public

## Information transferred

A 7-day probabilistic temperature forecast; the resulting alert level from a four-level colour-coded vocabulary; protective action content. The plan notes mortality risk rises markedly around 42 °C.

## Channels

Deliberately **low-tech and multi-channel**: e-mail for the internal forecast handoff; then "training workshops, hoardings/billboards, pamphlets, and print advertisements", thousands of pamphlets distributed via schoolchildren and urban health centres, "a radio campaign in local languages", and electronic temperature displays. **No app and no public dashboard.** The public-facing layer was built entirely on channels that require neither a smartphone nor an internet connection.

## Handoffs and ownership

The design principle worth importing: **a single named nodal officer is the designated recipient of the daily forecast and the coordinating point for the city's response**, and "the Health Department is responsible for overarching response coordination, including monitoring forecasts and sending heat health alerts." The plan "required formal, pre-established communication channels among agencies, responders, providers, community groups and media, activated ahead of forecast high temperatures" (§3.5.1) — the channels are agreed *before* the season, not improvised during it.

The last-mile human layer is quantified: **64 urban health centres, 26 hospitals and over 1,000 community link workers**.

## Acknowledgement or escalation

The colour-coded four-level vocabulary carries escalation. No acknowledgement or receipt-confirmation mechanism is described.

## Failure points

Self-reported by the implementers: **"Communication was often a challenge"** across time zones and languages. The plan's own stated improvement priorities were "improving formal communication channels" and designating clear agency roles *before* extreme heat events — i.e. the two things that were hardest were exactly ownership clarity and channel reliability.

## Adoption or satisfaction findings

None. The paper does not test outcomes and reports **no per-channel reach statistics** — it is a design description, not an effectiveness evaluation.

## Relevance to this project

Two directly importable design decisions: (a) name a single accountable recipient for the incoming risk signal rather than sending it to a distribution list; (b) hold the alert *vocabulary* constant across heterogeneous channels so a billboard, a radio spot and a dashboard tile carry the same level. It is also a useful counterweight to a screen-first assumption: the most-cited heat action plan in a hot, low-connectivity city put almost nothing on a screen.

## Limitations

Single-city descriptive case study; first year of operation; no controlled evaluation of the communication chain; **authorship includes the plan's own developers**, so favourable framing is a real risk; the custom forecast component was research-funded and may not be sustainable; the 2014 vintage predates widespread smartphone penetration in the study city.

## Supported claims

1. Ahmedabad's heat action plan designated a single municipal nodal officer as the named recipient of daily forecast alerts and the coordinating point for the city's heat response.
2. The alert cascade runs from forecast provider to municipal nodal officer, then Health Department, then local departments and community service providers, then the public via media, radio, billboards and printed materials (§3.5.2).
3. The plan identified 64 urban health centres, 26 hospitals and over 1,000 community link workers as the operational human resource for activation.
4. The plan required formal, pre-established communication channels among agencies, responders, providers, community groups and media, activated ahead of forecast high temperatures (§3.5.1).
5. Public reach was built on a deliberately low-tech multi-channel mix — billboards, pamphlets, local-language radio and electronic temperature displays — not apps or dashboards.
6. A four-level colour-coded alert vocabulary was used to keep the message consistent across heterogeneous channels.
7. The implementers reported that communication was often a challenge and prioritised improving formal communication channels and clarifying agency roles before extreme heat events.
