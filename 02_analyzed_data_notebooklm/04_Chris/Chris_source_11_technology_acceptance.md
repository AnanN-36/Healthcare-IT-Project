## Research context

CS-55 and CS-56 are the foundational technology-acceptance papers that define the constructs. CS-58 is a 2025 meta-analysis of **41 studies and 11,574 participants, mean age 67.58 (SD 4.76)** — the constructs measured in the exact demographic HeatShield most needs to reach.

## Stakeholders

## Failure points

The meta-analysis reports **substantial heterogeneity**, moderated by geographic region, technology type and the presence of visual demonstrations. Applying pooled correlations to a Thai context without local testing would over-claim.

## Adoption or satisfaction findings

The headline result, verbatim: **"Random-effects meta-analysis revealed significant positive correlations for PU-BI (r=0.607, 95% CI 0.543-0.665; P<.001), PEOU-BI (r=0.525, 95% CI 0.462-0.583; P<.001), and SI-BI (r=0.551, 95% CI 0.468-0.624; P<.001)."**

That is: for older adults, **perceived usefulness** is the strongest predictor of intention to use, **social influence** is close behind, and **perceived ease of use** is third. The meta-analysis also finds that **presenting a visual demonstration of the technology moderates acceptance** — relevant to onboarding design.

Two constructs are notable by their absence: **trust was not among the pooled constructs reported**, and the outcome is behavioural *intention*, not verified protective behaviour.

## Relevance to this project

Three consequences for the evaluation framework:

1. **Measure perceived usefulness first.** It has the strongest association with intention in exactly this population. A short prototype survey that measures only ease of use would be testing the weaker predictor.
2. **Measure social influence.** At r=0.551 it is nearly as strong as usefulness — which supports routing heat messages through community intermediaries (village health volunteers, family, community leaders) rather than only through an institutional channel. This converges independently with CS-61 and CS-34.
3. **Do not claim these instruments are validated for heat warnings.** No instrument located in this review has been. Adapted items must be labelled as adapted.

## Limitations

CS-58 is correlational, not causal; heterogeneity is substantial; the outcome is intention rather than behaviour; and only the structured abstract was read, not the forest plots or moderator tables. For CS-55 and CS-56, **full text was not accessed and the original item wording could not be verified**, so no TAM or UTAUT item is reproduced verbatim anywhere in the Chris deliverables — items derived from these models are labelled `ADAPTED` and their wording is the project's own.

## Supported claims

1. Among older adults using health care technology (41 studies, 11,574 participants), perceived usefulness correlated with behavioural intention at r=0.607 (95% CI 0.543–0.665; P<.001).
2. Perceived ease of use correlated with behavioural intention at r=0.525 (95% CI 0.462–0.583; P<.001).
3. Social influence correlated with behavioural intention at r=0.551 (95% CI 0.468–0.624; P<.001).
4. Heterogeneity was influenced by geographic region, technology type and the presence of visual demonstrations.
5. Perceived usefulness, perceived ease of use and behavioural intention originate as constructs in Davis (1989); performance expectancy, effort expectancy, social influence and facilitating conditions originate in Venkatesh et al. (2003).
6. The original item wording of the TAM and UTAUT instruments was not verified in this review and is not reproduced.
