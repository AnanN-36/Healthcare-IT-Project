# Chris Source 13 — Alert Fatigue and the "Cry Wolf" Effect: A Contested Evidence Base

**Focus applied to this summary:** stakeholders, communication logistics, contact points, channels, handoffs, ownership, acknowledgement, fallback mechanisms, adoption, satisfaction and service usefulness. Contact-point level only.

> **This summary deliberately does not resolve a disagreement.** Three bodies of evidence point in different directions, and the honest position is to present all three.

## Research context

Whether repeated or false alarms erode response is one of the most commonly assumed design constraints in alerting — and one of the least settled.

## Stakeholders

The alerted public; clinicians receiving device alarms; alert originators deciding how often to issue.

## Contact points / Channels / Handoffs

Not the focus. The relevant contact point is the repeated one: the same recipient receiving successive alerts across a season.

## Acknowledgement or escalation

CS-41 is the only source located that measures a **dose–response** relationship between prior alert exposure and subsequent responsiveness — the mechanism that "alert fatigue" names.

## The three positions

**Position 1 — Evidence *against* a cry-wolf effect in public warning.** CS-49, the best-powered direct test located (**N=4,162**), found that perceived false-alarm ratios were **not** correlated with actual county false-alarm ratios, and — counterintuitively — that **higher perceived false-alarm ratios were associated with *more* protective action**. Actual false-alarm ratios and warning counts did not predict protective action at all. CS-60, a federal synthesis of over 200 studies, lists **"the 'cry wolf' syndrome" explicitly among "myths about public response to emergency warning [that] are at odds with knowledge derived from field investigations"**, and names warning frequency and repetition as factors that *support* rather than erode response.

**Position 2 — Evidence *for* trust erosion.** Sawada et al. (2022) model repeated false alarms as carrying an implicit cost through erosion of collective trust, strongest where events are infrequent. Matzarakis & Giannaros (2025) warn of "public desensitization" — but this is asserted in an **editorial**, not measured. Chandra & Lee (2025) name "inadequate addressing of heat warning fatigue" as a gap in the heat-warning literature — i.e. they identify it as *unstudied*, not as demonstrated.

**Position 3 — Evidence *for* fatigue, but only in high-volume clinical alerting.** CS-41 found **"87.1% of PICU and 99.0% of ward clinical alarms were nonactionable"**, with response time rising incrementally with prior 120-minute non-actionable exposure — a measured dose–response. CS-40 (AHRQ) reports false alarms "range from 72 percent to 99 percent". These are the only direct empirical demonstrations of desensitization anywhere in this evidence base — **but they occur at volumes of tens of thousands of alarms per unit per fortnight, against a heat alert issued a handful of times per season.**

## Failure points

The design risk is symmetrical and worth stating plainly: **designing HeatShield around an assumed fatigue effect is unsupported, and asserting that fatigue has been disproven is equally unsupported.**

## Adoption or satisfaction findings

The relevant adoption finding is negative: **no heat-specific empirical test of warning fatigue was located by any workstream in this review.**

## Relevance to this project

1. Do not use a presumed fatigue effect to justify suppressing alerts or raising thresholds.
2. Do not transfer clinical false-alarm rates (72–99%) to a heat dashboard — the volume regimes are not comparable, and doing so would imply a heat-alert false-alarm rate that no source supports.
3. **Do** treat alert volume as an open question the prototype should measure. Because CS-46 shows flat prioritisation destroys signal-to-noise, tiering by urgency is defensible on ownership grounds even without a fatigue finding.
4. This is a legitimate and citable evidence gap for `Chris_literature_review.md`.

## Limitations

CS-49 is tornado (rapid-onset), single US region, cross-sectional and self-report. CS-41 and CS-40 are clinical device alarms. CS-60 is a 1990 synthesis whose channel conclusions are dated, and only the OSTI abstract page was accessed. Sawada et al. is modelling; Matzarakis & Giannaros is an editorial and is downgraded to framing only.

## Supported claims

1. In a study of 4,162 respondents, perceived false-alarm ratios were not correlated with actual county false-alarm ratios, and higher perceived false-alarm ratios were associated with more, not less, protective behaviour.
2. A federal synthesis of over 200 studies lists the "cry wolf" syndrome among myths about public warning response that are at odds with field investigation.
3. In a children's hospital, 87.1% of PICU and 99.0% of ward clinical alarms were non-actionable, and response time rose incrementally with prior non-actionable alarm exposure.
4. AHRQ reports clinical false-alarm rates ranging from 72% to 99%.
5. A 2025 systematic review of heat health warning systems names heat warning fatigue as an inadequately addressed gap.
6. No heat-specific empirical test of warning fatigue was located in this review.
